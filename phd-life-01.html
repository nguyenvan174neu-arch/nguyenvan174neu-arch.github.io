A PhD Is More Than a Degree

What doing a PhD abroad is teaching me about research, independence, adaptability, courage — and myself.
September 2026 · Van Nguyen Thi

Before starting my PhD, I thought I had a fairly good idea of what a PhD would involve.

Read papers. Develop research questions. Collect data. Run regressions. Write papers. Present them. Revise them.

And eventually, hopefully, receive a degree.

Sounds fairly straightforward.

Of course, that was before I discovered that a regression can look absolutely beautiful one evening, and then the next morning, after adding just one control variable, all the results can suddenly behave as if they have never met you before.

Somewhere along the way, I began to realise that doing a PhD abroad is teaching me much more than how to conduct research.

Research is only part of the journey

There have certainly been exciting moments in my PhD: finding an interesting result, presenting my work, receiving useful feedback, and seeing an idea slowly become a research paper.

But there have also been plenty of other moments.

Results that did not turn out as expected.

Conversations that were difficult.

Decisions that I wished somebody else could make for me.

Moments when I had to adapt to people whose ways of thinking, communicating, or working were very different from mine.

And days when I simply had to remind myself that one disappointing regression result was not a judgement of my ability as a researcher.

Perhaps a PhD is not only about learning how to answer research questions. It is also about learning how to respond when life does not give you the answer you expected.

Learning to become independent

Moving to another country for a PhD adds another layer to the experience.

You are not only learning how to become an independent researcher. You are also learning how to function in a new environment.

A different academic system.

A different culture.

A language that is not your mother tongue.

New professional relationships, new friendships, and sometimes new rules that nobody actually tells you are rules.

At first, I thought independence meant being able to solve everything by myself.

I do not think that anymore.

Independence means taking responsibility for my own decisions while also knowing when to ask for advice, when to listen, and when to accept help.

It means understanding that other people can guide me, support me, and care about me — but ultimately, I still need to decide which direction I want my own journey to take.

Learning to live with differences

Another lesson has been learning how to work with people who are not like me.

Sometimes very much not like me.

Different disciplines. Different cultures. Different personalities. Different communication styles. Different expectations about how things should be done.

When I first arrived, I probably thought successful collaboration meant finding people who thought in similar ways.

Now I think it means something quite different.

It means learning to understand how another person sees the world without necessarily having to see it exactly the same way.

It means listening carefully, communicating clearly, respecting differences, and still having the confidence to maintain your own perspective.

That skill may eventually be as useful to me as many of the technical skills I have learned during the PhD.

And sometimes, learning to be brave

There have also been moments when adapting was not enough.

Sometimes we need to make a decision.

And some decisions are uncomfortable precisely because they matter.

One of the things this journey has gradually taught me is that being respectful does not mean always saying yes.

Being adaptable does not mean accepting every situation indefinitely.

And being grateful does not mean giving up your own judgement about what you need in order to grow.

Sometimes courage is surprisingly quiet.

It is simply looking honestly at a situation and saying:

This is not working. Something needs to change.

I learned this lesson through one of the more difficult decisions in my own PhD journey.

But that story deserves an episode of its own.

So, what is a PhD really about?

I am still doing mine, so perhaps I should not pretend that I already know the complete answer.

Ask me again when I submit my thesis.

I may have a completely different answer by then.

But at this point in the journey, I know one thing.

A PhD abroad is not simply training me to become a better researcher.

It is teaching me to become more independent.

More adaptable.

More comfortable with uncertainty.

Better at communicating with people who are different from me.

More willing to make difficult decisions.

And hopefully, a little calmer when things do not go according to plan.

One day, the PhD degree may hang on my wall.

But perhaps the most important outcome of this journey will never fit inside a frame:

the person I became while earning it.
